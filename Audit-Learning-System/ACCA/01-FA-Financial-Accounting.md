# FA — Financial Accounting (ACCA Applied Knowledge)

> The foundation of everything. If you only truly master one ACCA paper before the
> others, make it this one. Auditing is impossible without it.

**ACCA level:** Applied Knowledge · **Exam:** on-demand, computer-based, 2 hours, 50% to pass.

---

## Why this matters for your audit job

Every audit checks numbers that were produced by the accounting process FA describes.
If you don't understand how a transaction becomes a journal, a ledger balance, a trial
balance, and finally a line in the financial statements — you can't check whether it's
right. **FA is the language. Everything else is built on it.**

---

## 1. The accounting equation (memorise this forever)

```
ASSETS = LIABILITIES + EQUITY
```

- **Assets** — things the business owns/controls that bring future benefit (cash, inventory, receivables, equipment).
- **Liabilities** — what the business owes (payables, loans, tax due).
- **Equity** — what's left for the owners (capital invested + profits kept in the business).

Every single transaction keeps this equation in balance. That's the whole secret of
double entry: **two sides, always equal.**

Expanded version:
```
ASSETS = LIABILITIES + (CAPITAL + PROFIT − DRAWINGS)
where PROFIT = INCOME − EXPENSES
```

---

## 2. Double entry: debits and credits

Every transaction has **two effects** — one debit (Dr) and one credit (Cr), and they
must be equal in money terms.

The classic memory aid is **DEAD CLIC**:

| DEBIT increases | CREDIT increases |
|-----------------|------------------|
| **D**rawings | **C**apital |
| **E**xpenses | **L**iabilities |
| **A**ssets | **I**ncome |
| **D**ividends | (and reduces the **C** above) |

So:
- Buy equipment for cash → **Dr Equipment** (asset up), **Cr Cash** (asset down).
- Make a sale on credit → **Dr Receivables** (asset up), **Cr Sales** (income up).
- Pay rent → **Dr Rent expense** (expense up), **Cr Cash** (asset down).
- Take a bank loan → **Dr Cash** (asset up), **Cr Loan** (liability up).

> **Test yourself:** If totals of all debits don't equal totals of all credits, you made
> an error. This is exactly the kind of check auditors rely on.

## 3. The three financial statements

These are the outputs of accounting and the **subject of every audit**.

### a) Statement of Financial Position (SOFP) — "the balance sheet"
A snapshot at one date. Shows Assets, Liabilities, and Equity. It must *balance*
(left = right, per the accounting equation).

Structure:
- **Non-current assets** (used long-term: property, plant, equipment, intangibles)
- **Current assets** (turned to cash within a year: inventory, receivables, cash)
- **Current liabilities** (due within a year: payables, short-term loans, tax)
- **Non-current liabilities** (long-term loans)
- **Equity** (share capital, retained earnings)

### b) Statement of Profit or Loss (SOPL) — "the income statement"
Performance over a *period*. 

```
Revenue
− Cost of sales
= Gross profit
− Operating expenses
= Operating profit
− Finance costs
± Tax
= Profit for the year
```

### c) Statement of Cash Flows
Where cash actually came from and went, split into:
- **Operating** activities (day-to-day trading)
- **Investing** activities (buying/selling long-term assets)
- **Financing** activities (loans, share issues, dividends)

> Profit ≠ Cash. A company can be profitable but run out of cash. Understanding this
> distinction is a recurring audit and exam theme.

## 4. The accounting cycle (journal → statements)

```
1. Transaction occurs (invoice, payment, etc.)
        ↓
2. Recorded as a JOURNAL entry (Dr / Cr)
        ↓
3. Posted to LEDGER accounts (one account per item)
        ↓
4. Balances listed in the TRIAL BALANCE (all Drs should = all Crs)
        ↓
5. ADJUSTMENTS made (accruals, prepayments, depreciation, etc.)
        ↓
6. FINANCIAL STATEMENTS prepared
```

As an auditor, you trace this chain **backwards**: from a number in the statements down
to the supporting evidence (the invoice, the contract, the bank statement).

## 5. Key adjustments you must understand

These are where most accounting *judgement* lives — and therefore where audit attention focuses.

| Adjustment | What it does | Example |
|-----------|--------------|---------|
| **Accruals** | Record an expense incurred but not yet paid/invoiced | Electricity used in Dec, billed in Jan |
| **Prepayments** | Remove an expense paid in advance for a future period | Insurance paid for next year |
| **Depreciation** | Spread the cost of a non-current asset over its useful life | Machine costing 10,000 over 10 years → 1,000/yr |
| **Irrecoverable debts** | Write off receivables that won't be collected | A customer goes bankrupt |
| **Allowance for receivables** | Estimate of receivables that *might* not pay | 5% of year-end receivables |
| **Inventory valuation** | Value at lower of cost and net realisable value (IAS 2) | Damaged goods written down |
| **Closing inventory** | Adjust cost of sales for unsold goods | Counted at year-end |

**Depreciation methods:**
- **Straight line:** `(Cost − Residual value) ÷ Useful life` — equal charge each year.
- **Reducing balance:** `Carrying amount × fixed %` — higher charge early, lower later.

## 6. Other core FA topics

- **Bank reconciliations** — match the cash book to the bank statement; explain differences (unpresented cheques, outstanding lodgements, bank charges). *You will do these in Excel constantly.*
- **Control accounts** — total accounts for receivables and payables, reconciled to the detailed lists.
- **Correction of errors & suspense accounts** — fixing mistakes so the trial balance balances.
- **Sales tax / VAT** — accounting for tax collected on behalf of the government.
- **Accounting for limited companies** — share capital, share premium, reserves, dividends.
- **Incomplete records** — reconstructing figures when full records don't exist.

---

## 7. The qualitative characteristics of good information

Useful financial information is:
- **Relevant** — can influence decisions.
- **Faithfully represented** — complete, neutral, free from error.

Enhanced by: **Comparability, Verifiability, Timeliness, Understandability.**

> These same ideas underpin *why* an audit exists — to give assurance the information is
> faithfully represented.

## 8. How auditors use FA every day

| FA concept | On the audit |
|------------|--------------|
| Trial balance | The starting point of the audit file; agrees to the financial statements. |
| Double entry | Lets you predict the "other side" of any transaction and spot what's missing. |
| Bank reconciliation | A standard audit working paper. |
| Accruals/prepayments | Tested for completeness and cut-off (right period). |
| Depreciation | Recalculated to check the charge is reasonable. |
| Receivables allowance | Challenged as a key estimate/judgement. |

## 9. Self-test questions

1. A business buys inventory on credit for 2,000. What are the debit and credit entries?
2. State the accounting equation and rearrange it to solve for equity.
3. Explain the difference between an accrual and a prepayment with an example of each.
4. A machine costs 50,000, has a 5-year life and 5,000 residual value. What is the annual straight-line depreciation?
5. Why can a profitable company still run out of cash?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Dr Inventory 2,000 / Cr Payables 2,000.
2. Assets = Liabilities + Equity → Equity = Assets − Liabilities.
3. Accrual = expense incurred but not yet paid (liability created). Prepayment = expense paid in advance for a future period (asset created). E.g. accrual: unpaid electricity; prepayment: insurance for next year.
4. (50,000 − 5,000) ÷ 5 = **9,000 per year**.
5. Because profit includes non-cash items (credit sales not yet collected, depreciation) and excludes cash items like asset purchases and loan repayments. Cash and profit are different things.
</details>
